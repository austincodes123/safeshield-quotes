Why the number lands where it does

The reason coverage matters most now is the reason it won't matter forever.

You're early in two long, high-earning careers. The largest asset either of you holds isn't sitting in an account yet, it's the decades of income still ahead. Here's what the calculator is really protecting.

Income-rich before asset-rich

A physician and an engineer at these ages are sitting on enormous future earnings, likely well into eight figures across two careers, that haven't converted into savings yet. That future income is the most valuable thing either of you owns, and life insurance protects it until it becomes financial capital.

Existing assets are a floor, not a replacement

Everything you've saved and been left counts, and the calculator credits every dollar of it. What those assets can't do is stand in for 20-plus years of a doctor's or an engineer's income on top of a mortgage and raising kids in this area. The gap between the two is what a policy covers.

The obligations are about to climb

A Westchester purchase in the next year or few means a large new mortgage, and often kids not far behind. Coverage need tends to peak right around this stage of life, which is also the moment when locking a low rate pays off the most.

You'll never be more insurable than now

Two people in excellent health at these ages qualify for the best class a carrier offers, and that rate locks level for the entire term. Every year forward, the same coverage costs more, and health is never more certain than it is today.

Why term, not whole life

For you specifically, whole life is an expensive answer to a question you don't have.

Whole life isn't a scam, it has real uses: estate liquidity for the ultra-wealthy, business buy-sell, special-needs planning, forced savings for people who won't invest on their own. None of those is you. Here's the math that decides it.

Same death benefit the entire time. But the difference stays yours: liquid, controllable, and compounding in your name instead of building slow cash value inside a policy you'd pay roughly $600,000 into over 30 years.

You already have better tax buckets

The "tax-advantaged growth" pitch for whole life ignores that you two aren't maxing 401(k)s, backdoor Roths, an HSA, and 529s for the kids yet. Those beat whole life's internal return, and low-cost index funds in a brokerage account beat it after that. Fill the good buckets first.

Your need is temporary by design

Permanent insurance solves a permanent problem. Yours isn't. Once the mortgage is paid, the kids are launched, and your investments have compounded, the need is simply gone. Paying lifelong premiums to insure a risk that expires is the expensive part.

Why 30 years is the right term

The one chart that explains the whole strategy.

Your need for coverage falls every year as the mortgage shrinks and the kids grow up. Your net worth climbs every year as you save and invest. Around your mid-50s, the lines cross and you're self-insured. A 30-year term is built to cover exactly the gap between those two lines, and then get out of your way.

It matches your actual liabilities

A 30-year mortgage taken next year is paid off right as the term ends. Kids born in the next few years finish college with years to spare. The term is engineered to the exact shape of your obligations, not a round number.

Shorter costs more in the long run

A 20-year term would expire at 50 and 51, with the mortgage possibly still running and kids maybe still in school. Re-buying then costs multiples more and depends on your health at 50. Locking 30 years now buys certainty at the lowest price you'll ever see.
