Why not 10 or 15

The coverage runs out while your youngest is still in high school. That is the worst possible year to be uninsured, because the need is at its peak and replacement coverage at that age costs materially more than it does today.

Why not 30

At 66 the boys are in their thirties, the mortgage is long gone, and the portfolio has had three more decades to compound. You would be paying every single month to protect a dependency that no longer exists.

Why 20 lands right

It carries you to 56. The youngest is out of college, the mortgage is retired or nearly so, and the assets that replace your income are already sitting in your accounts. The coverage retires the same year the need does.

03 / Why term, in your case specifically

Permanent insurance solves a problem you do not have

I want to be careful here, because whole life and indexed universal life are not scams and there are households where they genuinely fit. Yours is not one of them, and the reason is specific to you rather than a slogan.

What permanent is actually for

Not you
  • A need that never ends, such as a special needs child or an estate tax bill that must be paid in cash on the day of death
  • A household that will not otherwise accumulate assets, where forced saving inside a policy beats no saving at all
  • Business succession, where a buy-sell agreement has to fund on an unknown date
  • Someone who has already maxed every qualified plan and is genuinely out of tax-advantaged room

What is actually true about your household

Term
  • Two strong incomes, a real savings rate, and an inheritance ahead of you. You are going to build the assets. You do not need a life insurance company to make you do it
  • Your need has an end date and you can see it on the bars above. Three boys, twenty years, done
  • The premium gap is not marginal. Permanent coverage at these face amounts typically runs many multiples of term for the same death benefit
  • Every dollar you do not hand to a carrier is a dollar in your own accounts, at your cost basis, under your control
The honest catch, stated plainly: term expires. If you are 56 and still want coverage, you will be buying it at 56 year old rates, and if your health has changed by then you may not be able to buy it at all. That is a real risk and I am not going to wave it away. It is also why both policies I am showing you are convertible, which means you can exchange them for permanent coverage later without a new medical exam. You are not locked out. You just are not paying for permanent coverage today that you do not need today.

Choosing between the two carriers

Symetra SwiftTerm

  • Rated A by AM Best. Strong balance sheet, well established, not a name anyone should be nervous about
  • Convertible, same as Principal. You keep the right to exchange into permanent coverage later with no new medical underwriting
  • Instant issue eligible. For two healthy 36 year olds that can mean no paramed exam and coverage in force in days rather than six to eight weeks
  • Lower premium in every band, for both of you. You are saving per year at the selected amounts

Principal National

  • Rated A+ by AM Best, one notch above Symetra. That is the entire case for it, and it is a legitimate one
  • Convertible, same feature set
  • Full underwriting. Expect a paramed exam and a longer path to in force
  • Costs more per year at the selected amounts, for a rating difference that matters far less on a 20 year term than it would on a policy meant to pay 50 years from now
Where I land, and you can tell me I am wrong. Symetra. Both carriers are convertible, so Principal's old tiebreaker is gone. What is left is A versus A+ against a lower premium and the ability to get you covered in days instead of weeks. On a 20 year term, the gap between an A and an A+ carrier is a rounding error next to the risk of sitting uninsured for two months while the paperwork grinds. If the A+ matters to you, take Principal and I will write it happily. It is a good policy and I will not argue with you.
05 / My recommendation

If you asked me at the kitchen table

You did not come to a call center, so here is a straight answer with the reasoning attached, which means you can disagree with any single piece of it.

Todd at $5M

Twenty years of a $400,000 income is the largest asset this household owns, and right now it is insured only by a group policy that vanishes the day he leaves the hospital. At roughly $202 a month, this is the cheapest thing on the page relative to what it is protecting.

Leah at $3M

The reflex is to insure the bigger earner and stop. With three boys under ten that is a mistake. If Leah is gone, Todd is replacing $250,000 a year plus childcare and the running of a household, on a physician's schedule. $3M is the honest number, not a courtesy.

Both, at the same time

Buy them together, through the same brokerage, on the same day. The failure mode I see constantly is one spouse covered and the other one meaning to get around to it. It is completely avoidable and it is avoidable right now.
