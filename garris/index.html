What a “table rating” actually means

Rated is not declined. It's a price, and the price is on this page.

When underwriting sees something in a file it wants to charge for, it doesn't have to say no — it applies a “table.” Your quotes are built at Table 3 and Table 4 so you can see the realistic range before you ever apply. Here's how to read them.

The tables, in plain English

Carriers price rated cases in steps above their standard rate, roughly +25% per table. Table 3 (also called “C”) is about 75% above standard; Table 4 (“D”) is about double. That's the entire mechanism — same policy, same guarantees, same death benefit, adjusted price.

You're seeing the bracket, not a guess

Both carriers are quoted at Table 3 and Table 4, side by side. If underwriting lands at Table 3, you pay the lower row. If it lands at Table 4, the worst case was already on this page. And if it comes back better than Table 3, every number here only improves.

Why locking it now beats waiting

The rating you get today is a floor you can improve. Waiting has no floor at all.

The instinct after a rough underwriting experience is to wait for a better moment. For a rated case, the math runs the other way — and the industry has a built-in mechanism most people never hear about.

Apply now, improve later

Carriers allow a policyholder to request reconsideration of the rating — typically after about a year — with updated evidence. If your health picture improves, we ask for a re-rate on the same policy and the premium drops. You stay covered the whole time. The reverse is not available: an uninsured year of worsening health cannot be bought back.

Every birthday raises the base

A table rating is a multiplier on the carrier's standard rate for your age. Wait, and the base the table multiplies goes up — so even an identical rating next year costs more, and at 40 the age curve is steepening, not flattening.

An offer in hand changes the negotiation

With a firm offer issued, I can shop it. Carriers will sometimes beat a competitor's issued class to win a case — but only when there's a real offer to beat. Getting to “yes” once creates leverage that “still shopping” never does.

The guarantee outlasts the reason for it

Once issued, the policy is locked: the carrier can never re-underwrite you, raise the rate, or cancel for health reasons. The thing that made coverage hard to get has no power over coverage you already own.

The case for 20

If the mortgage and the heaviest obligations resolve by your late 50s, a 20-year term covers the real gap at a meaningfully lower price, and the savings can go straight into the investments that make the gold line climb faster.

The case for 30

A 20-year term expires at 60 — and for a rated case, re-buying at 60 means new underwriting at whatever your health is then. If there's any chance the need outlives 60, the 30-year lock is the version of this decision you never have to revisit.
